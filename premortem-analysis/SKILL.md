# Premortem Analysis + Structured Self-Critique

*Source: Pherson & Heuer, Structured Analytic Techniques for Intelligence Analysis, 3rd ed., §8.2.2–8.2.3 — adapted from Gary Klein (1998) and Heuer & Pherson (2019)*

## Purpose

These two techniques are designed to be run together, in sequence, to expose weaknesses in an analysis **before** it is finalized — preventing the need for a postmortem later.

- **Premortem Analysis** is the right-brained technique. It uses reframing to free the mind from defensive reasoning: project forward to a future moment where the analysis has turned out to be spectacularly wrong, then work backward to explain how. This open-ended brainstorm surfaces problems that structured questioning alone might miss, and it *legitimizes dissent* within a group — team members contribute by being critical, not by supporting the consensus.

- **Structured Self-Critique (SSC)** is the left-brained companion. After the Premortem, analysts don a "black hat" and systematically probe ten specific dimensions of analytic failure. It is the structured mechanism that ensures nothing is overlooked; the Premortem is the creative spark that ensures the SSC is not just going through the motions.

Run them in the order below. If only one technique is requested — or if time is short — Premortem alone is still valuable; SSC alone is still valuable. But together they cover the full space of potential error.

---

## Step 1: Ingest the input

Accept any of the following:
- **Analysis or assessment in chat** — a summary, key judgment, or estimate the analyst wants to challenge
- **Pasted product or draft** — extract the key judgment(s), key assumptions, and evidence base
- **Uploaded document** — read and identify the primary analytic conclusions to stress-test
- **Prior SAT output** — e.g., a Key Assumptions Check or What If? Analysis whose conclusions now need a final challenge round

If the input is vague ("stress test my analysis"), ask a **single short clarifying message** covering:
1. What is the key judgment or conclusion being stress-tested?
2. How confident is the analyst/team in that judgment, and why?
3. Output format: inline in chat (quick debrief), HTML (structured deliverable), or Word .docx (formal product)?

If the answer is obvious from context, skip asking and proceed, noting assumptions.

---

## Step 2: Run the Premortem

### Set the frame

Restate the key judgment clearly, then apply the Premortem reframe — out loud, in writing:

> *"We now believe [key judgment]. Our report will go forward and be accepted. Now imagine that [6 / 12 / 18] months from now, we learn from an unimpeachable source that our analysis was completely wrong. Things turned out very differently than we expected. Working from that future vantage point — what could possibly have happened to cause our analysis to be spectacularly wrong?"*

Choose a time horizon that is realistic for the issue at hand. The emotional vividness of the future failure is what activates different reasoning. Do not soften this frame — the analysis needs to be *spectacularly* wrong, not merely slightly off.

### Generate failure modes

Brainstorm freely. Do not filter or rank at this stage — the goal is volume and variety. Consider failure modes across at least these dimensions:

- **Evidence failures**: Key reporting turned out to be fabricated, fabricated by an adversary, misread, or outdated. What we thought was diagnostic wasn't.
- **Assumption failures**: A foundational assumption that was treated as given turned out to be false. Which assumption, if wrong, most directly invalidates the judgment?
- **Cognitive/process failures**: The team anchored on an early assessment, discounted dissenting views, succumbed to mirror-imaging, or missed an emerging trend because it didn't fit the prevailing model.
- **External shocks**: An independent development — technological, political, economic, environmental — changed the operating environment in ways the analysis did not anticipate.
- **Actor behavior failures**: The foreign government, adversary, or competitor did not behave rationally or predictably. A different decision model (bureaucratic politics, organizational routine, authoritarian whim) better explains what happened.
- **Information gaps**: The absence of reporting on something was misread as confirmation of the prevailing judgment rather than as a collection gap.
- **Deception**: The adversary or subject successfully manipulated what the team saw, believed, or emphasized.

For **solo use**: Write out as many failure modes as possible before reviewing them. The goal is to generate items you would not have produced if thinking defensively.

For **group use**: Use the Nominal Group Technique — go around the room in round-robin fashion, each participant contributing one failure mode at a time until all lists are exhausted. Record everything visibly. No filtering during collection.

### Discuss and cluster

After generating failure modes, group similar items and surface the **most dangerous** ones — those that, if they materialized, would most completely overturn the key judgment. Look especially for:

- Failure modes that multiple team members raised independently (convergent concern)
- Failure modes that nobody wanted to raise but that are analytically valid (suppressed dissent)
- Failure modes that are uncomfortable because they implicate the team's own prior work

### Decision point

At the end of the Premortem:

**If no significant failure modes emerged**: Note this explicitly. State why the judgment appears robust. Consider whether the team was genuinely stress-testing or unconsciously protecting the conclusion. If confident the Premortem was rigorous, the Structured Self-Critique can be brief.

**If one or more significant failure modes emerged**: Proceed to the Structured Self-Critique to investigate them systematically. The Premortem has done its job by flagging that a problem exists; the SSC diagnoses what the problem is and how serious it is.

---

## Step 3: Run the Structured Self-Critique

All analysts now don the black hat. They are no longer advocates for the analysis — they are critics of it. Their job is to find weaknesses, not to defend the previous judgment. This role shift is not cosmetic; it needs to be explicit and sustained throughout.

Work through each of the following ten dimensions. For each dimension, record:
- What the team found (even if "no concern here")
- Any weaknesses identified
- The impact on confidence in the judgment if the weakness is real

---

### Dimension 1 — Sources of Uncertainty

Identify what kind of problem the team is actually dealing with:

- **Is this a puzzle or a mystery?** A puzzle has a correct answer that better collection can reveal. A mystery has no single correct answer — it depends on future interactions among many factors, some unknown. Puzzles warrant high confidence if collection is good; mysteries warrant structural humility regardless of evidence quality.
- How does the team rate the **quality and timeliness** of its evidence?
- Are there an **unusually large number of assumptions** relative to the evidence? (This signals a mystery masquerading as a puzzle.)
- Is the situation **stable or rapidly changing**? Rapidly changing situations make past-pattern analysis unreliable.

---

### Dimension 2 — Analytic Process

Probe whether the analysis was conducted rigorously:

- Did the team **identify alternative hypotheses** and actively seek evidence on them — or did it primarily collect evidence consistent with the prevailing view?
- Did the team **identify key assumptions** explicitly? (If not, run a Key Assumptions Check now.)
- Did the team seek **diverse opinions** — from other offices, agencies, academics, or practitioners who might see the problem differently?

If any of these steps were skipped, the odds of faulty analysis increase. Either take those steps now or lower confidence accordingly.

---

### Dimension 3 — Critical Assumptions

Identify the one or two assumptions that, if wrong, would most directly invalidate the key judgment. For each:

- How **recent and well-documented** is the evidence that supports this assumption?
- Brainstorm specific circumstances that could make this assumption false.
- If the assumption is false, does that support an **alternative hypothesis**?

If the team has not previously done a Key Assumptions Check, do one now — the SSC cannot adequately cover this dimension without it.

---

### Dimension 4 — Diagnostic Evidence

Identify which evidence is most responsible for distinguishing the key judgment from alternative hypotheses:

- What are the **most diagnostic pieces of evidence** — the items that, if removed or reinterpreted, would most weaken the judgment?
- For each diagnostic item: brainstorm **reasonable alternative interpretations** that could make it consistent with an alternative hypothesis rather than the leading one.
- Is the team's confidence in the judgment riding on a small number of high-diagnostic items that may not be as discriminating as assumed?

---

### Dimension 5 — Information Gaps

Probe the limits of what is known:

- Are there **gaps in available information** that the analysis papers over?
- Is some information so **dated** it may no longer be valid in a changed environment?
- Is the **absence of reporting** on a topic being treated as confirmation of the prevailing judgment, when it may simply reflect a collection gap?

---

### Dimension 6 — Missing Evidence

Apply an absence-of-evidence test:

- If the key judgment is correct, what **evidence would one expect to see** in the regular flow of intelligence or open-source reporting — that is **not there**?
- Is the absence of that expected evidence adequately explained?
- Could the lack of expected confirming evidence be a signal that the judgment is wrong, rather than simply a collection shortfall?

---

### Dimension 7 — Anomalous Evidence

Look for the clue that was discarded:

- Is there any **anomalous item of evidence** that was set aside as not important, not credible, or not relevant — but that, on reflection, could be a key signal pointing toward an alternative explanation?
- If that anomalous item were credible and relevant, which **alternative hypothesis** would it support?
- Anomalous evidence is disproportionately valuable precisely because it did not fit the prevailing model. Re-examine anything that was dismissed as "noise."

---

### Dimension 8 — Changes in the Broad Environment

Consider whether the world has changed around the analysis:

- Are there **social, technological, economic, environmental, or political developments** (STEEP) occurring independently of the issue under study that could affect whether the judgment proves correct?
- Is the team's analytic framework capturing a **past equilibrium** that may no longer hold?
- What **key forces or events** could occur in the external environment that would change the outcome regardless of the actors' intentions or capabilities?

---

### Dimension 9 — Alternative Decision Models

If the analysis involves judgment about behavior by a foreign government or non-state actor:

- Is the judgment based on a **rational actor assumption** — that the foreign party will pursue its interests coherently and efficiently?
- If so, consider whether the behavior could better be explained by:
  - **Bureaucratic bargaining** — the outcome of internal institutional competition, not a unified decision
  - **Organizational routine** — standard operating procedures that produced this outcome by default, not by design
  - **Authoritarian or personal whim** — a leader acting on idiosyncratic preferences, loyalty calculations, or emotional factors

If information for a more nuanced model is lacking, that itself lowers justified confidence in the judgment.

---

### Dimension 10 — Deception

Ask whether the analysis may have been manipulated:

- Does any country, organization, or competitor about which the team is making judgments have **motive, opportunity, and means** to conduct denial, deception, or influence operations?
- Does the subject have a **history** of such operations?
- Is there any pattern in what the team has been able to collect that looks suspiciously convenient or unusually coherent — as if designed to be seen?
- Apply the classic deception detection test: *If an adversary wanted us to reach exactly the conclusion we've reached, what would they have needed to do?* Does the available evidence pattern match what that would look like?

---

## Step 4: Reassess confidence

After completing all ten SSC dimensions, take off the black hat and return to the role of analyst.

Explicitly state a revised confidence judgment:

- **Reaffirmed**: The critique found no material weaknesses. The key judgment stands; confidence is unchanged or modestly increased.
- **Reaffirmed with caveats**: The judgment stands, but specific vulnerabilities were identified. State what evidence or developments would shift the judgment and at what threshold.
- **Revised downward**: The critique identified significant weaknesses. Lower confidence explicitly and describe what additional collection, analysis, or time would be needed to restore it.
- **Revised judgment**: The critique identified a failure mode severe enough to change the key judgment itself. State the revised judgment.

Do not allow the Premortem or SSC to become a rubber stamp. If weaknesses were found, they must appear in the output — including in the confidence statement.

---

## Step 5: Report the output

Structure the output as follows:

---

### Required output structure

**Header**: Analytic question | Assessment date | Analyst (if provided)

**Section 1 — Key Judgment Under Review**
State the conclusion being stress-tested, with a brief description of the evidence base and prior confidence level.

**Section 2 — Premortem Results**
List of failure modes generated, clustered by type. Flag the most dangerous ones. Note whether any failure modes were surfaced that had previously been suppressed or overlooked.

**Section 3 — Structured Self-Critique Findings**
For each of the ten dimensions: a brief statement of what the critique found. If a dimension was clean, say so in one sentence. If weaknesses were found, describe them specifically.

**Section 4 — Revised Confidence Assessment**
Explicit statement of whether the judgment is reaffirmed, reaffirmed with caveats, revised downward, or changed. If caveats or revisions apply, state the specific conditions or evidence that would shift the judgment further.

**Section 5 — Recommended Actions**
Any immediate follow-on steps: collection gaps to fill, assumptions to validate, alternative hypotheses to revisit, or companion techniques to run (Key Assumptions Check, Analysis of Competing Hypotheses, What If? Analysis, Deception Detection).

## Calibration notes

**On the Premortem**: The exercise only works if participants genuinely imagine the future failure and reason backward from it — not if they defensively search for reasons the analysis is correct. The facilitator should insist on this framing. A single analyst should be equally honest with herself.

**On the SSC**: The ten dimensions are a checklist, not a ritual. Experienced analysts who have internalized these questions during initial analysis will move through the SSC quickly. The time investment is proportional to how much of this thinking was *not* done earlier.

**On confidence calibration**: The goal is not to destroy confidence in every judgment — that would be paralysis. The goal is *accurate* confidence. If the Premortem and SSC find nothing materially wrong, the confidence level should reflect that. If they find serious problems, the confidence level must come down. Intellectual honesty here is what separates these techniques from theater.

**Relationship to other SATs**: Premortem Analysis and SSC are most powerful when preceded by a Key Assumptions Check (which feeds directly into SSC Dimension 3) and when followed by an Analysis of Competing Hypotheses if the SSC reveals a credible alternative explanation that was not previously taken seriously.
